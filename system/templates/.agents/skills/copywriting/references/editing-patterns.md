# Editing patterns and examples

Use these signals in context. The examples are invented illustrations, not
facts to reuse in a client's copy. They show the kind of decision to make,
not sentence templates or a phrase blacklist.

## Empty setup and staged sincerity

Before: "In today's fast-paced world, managing reservations is more important
than ever. Let's explore a smarter approach."
After: "See your reservations for the evening on one screen."
Use the latter only if the product actually provides that view. The edit replaces
an announcement with information. Likewise remove "Here's the thing" or
"Honestly?" when they stage a routine point; ordinary conversational asides can
stay when they belong to the writer.

## Contrasts with no useful negative half

Before: "It's not just a proposal tool. It's your growth engine."
After: "Create a proposal from your saved services and prices."
A real distinction can be useful: "The client can comment on a proposal, but
only your team can change its price." Both halves carry information. Keep it.

## Vague claims and unsupported authority

Before: "Unlock seamless collaboration with our industry-leading platform."
After: "Two editors can work on the same draft at once."
If simultaneous editing is unconfirmed, do not infer it from "collaboration."
Ask what collaborators can actually do. Replace "studies show" with the supplied
source and scoped finding, or flag the evidence gap. Never manufacture a statistic
to make a vague claim sound specific.

## Dramatic fragments and forced symmetry

Before: "Fewer tabs. More focus. Better work. Every single day."
After: "Review the invoice and its supporting receipts in the same view."
A short headline or a deliberate fragment can work. The issue is repeated
emphasis without new information. Do not fix it by forcing every sentence into
a different length or every section into a deliberately lopsided shape.

## Repeated closers and borrowed wisdom

Before: "Your team can approve the draft in the editor. Because great work
starts with great collaboration."
After: "Your team can approve the draft in the editor."
End when the point is complete. A next step or consequence earns its place when
it adds something the reader needs. Do not append a slogan to every section.

## Punctuation, lists and formatting by habit

Check whether repeated interruptions, bold labels or parallel bullet openers
help readers navigate or merely create a formula. Use prose for a connected
argument and lists for genuinely parallel items or steps. Keep three items when
there are three useful items. Follow project punctuation rules; a single dash or
formal word is not evidence of weak writing.

## Tone that exceeds the event

Before: "We're thrilled to unveil a revolutionary new way to reset your password!"
After: "You can now reset your password from the sign-in screen."
Match enthusiasm to the brand and occasion. A launch can be celebratory without
claiming a routine feature changes an industry. Do not add fake embarrassment,
confessions or customer stories to create relatability.

## Voice worth keeping

Original: "I built this because our estimates kept disappearing into spreadsheets."
Keep the concrete phrasing if it is the writer's true account. Do not polish it
into "Our solution was developed to streamline estimation workflows."
A sample's unusual word choice may matter more than grammatical neatness.
Preserve honest limits and uncertainty, especially in technical or factual copy.

## Meaning-preservation check

Original: "On the annual plan, eligible teams can request up to two onboarding calls."
Bad edit: "Every team gets two onboarding calls."
Better edit: "Eligible teams on the annual plan can request up to two onboarding calls."
The condition, eligibility, request requirement and limit all survive. Check the
same boundaries around trial terms, pricing, availability, guarantees and outcomes.
